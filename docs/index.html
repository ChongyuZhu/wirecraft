A Simulation Benchmark for Industrial DLO Manipulation

01 / Overview

TL;DR

WireCraft benchmarks connector insertion, clip routing, and channel seating in configurable simulation. Privileged state policies exceed 82% success in a representative setting of each task family; image-based policies lag behind. Adding simulated demonstrations improves physical UR5 task success by 15–65 percentage points in the best mixture for each task.

Abstract

Deformable Linear Objects (DLOs), such as wires and cables, are central to industrial assembly. Unlike rigid objects, whose state is captured by a 6-DoF pose, DLOs have an infinite-dimensional configuration space and deform continuously under contact with grippers, fixtures, and the workspace, making them a demanding benchmark for general dexterous manipulation. Despite their importance, policy development and comparison remain difficult: existing benchmarks are often tied to specific hardware setups, lack modular and customizable task assets, or study generic deformable-object tasks without the fixtures relevant to real-world industrial wire manipulation. Few benchmarks align simulation, real-world data, and shared evaluation protocols. To bridge this gap, we introduce WireCraft, a simulation benchmark for industrial DLO manipulation with configurable difficulty and assets, spanning three task families: connector insertion, clip routing, and channel seating. It supports two complementary DLO physics models, articulated and deformable, and the released trajectories come from both simulation and a physical UR5. We benchmark reinforcement learning (RL), imitation learning (IL), and vision-language-action (VLA) policies under shared metrics. Privileged state-based RL solves a representative setting in each task family with over 82% success, confirming the tasks are well-posed. Image-based policies remain less successful across the evaluated tasks, with ACT and DiT VLA falling below privileged State PPO. On a physical UR5, supplementing real demonstrations with simulated data improves success by 15–65 percentage points in the best-performing mixture for each task. These results indicate that industrial DLO manipulation, though tractable under privileged state, remains an open challenge for current vision-based learning.

TASK 01

Connector insertion

Grasp. Align. Insert.

Grasp the connector at the end of a wire, align it with a socket, and complete the insertion. Connector geometry and socket clearance control the difficulty.

Clip routing

Guide the wire through the clip.

Route a flexible wire through one or more clips. The benchmark varies the number of clips and their placement; the hardware evaluation uses one clip.

Physical UR5 · three views · original playback speed

Success criterionThe wire is seated in the target clip or clips.

Channel seating

Make the wire follow the channel.

Lay a wire inside a channel along its length. Straight and curved channels test shape control; the hardware evaluation uses a straight channel.

03 / Inside the benchmark

From task design to evaluation.

Explore the paper ↗

Built on Isaac Sim 4.5 and Isaac Lab 2.2.1, WireCraft combines industrial task fixtures with articulated rigid-body and finite-element deformable wire models. A shared dataset schema connects simulated and physical demonstrations to policy training.

Configurable physical tasks

Vary connector geometry and socket clearance, clip layout, or channel shape. The same task definitions are used to evaluate policy success.

Two wire representations

Articulated rigid-body chains support parallel data generation; finite-element deformable wires model local bending and contact deformation.

Shared learning interfaces

Reinforcement learning, imitation learning, and vision-language-action policies use task-specific observations. UR5 trajectories share a LeRobot v3.0 format.

04 / Evaluation

What the benchmark reveals.

Simulation

Reaching the socket is only part of insertion.

Privileged state policies solve the insertion task. Image-based policies lose success between reaching the socket and seating the connector, where alignment and contact matter.

The gap extends to routing and seating.

Action Chunking with Transformers (ACT) and a Diffusion Transformer vision-language-action policy (DiT VLA) remain below State Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) on these simulated tasks.

Physical robot

Simulation data helps on the UR5.

Adding simulated demonstrations improves observed task success on the physical UR5. The best mixture for each task increases success by 65 percentage points for insertion, 20 for routing, and 15 for seating, compared with real demonstrations alone.

What these results cover

The hardware study uses a UR5 with one fixture configuration per task and narrower randomization than simulation. Training-set size changes with data mixture, so these results do not isolate source diversity from data volume. They do not measure zero-shot transfer.
